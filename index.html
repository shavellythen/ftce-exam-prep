Prehistoric cultures through 20th century global events. Includes world religions. 13 skills — prehistoric to contemporary issues.

Your B.Sc. Economics is a massive advantage here. Supply/demand, macro basics, global trade, personal finance. 7 skills.
15% Political Science ~12 questions
U.S. Constitution, branches of government, elections, foreign policy, citizenship rights. 7 skills.

Six essential elements, map reading, human-environment interaction. 4 skills — most manageable section.

Primary vs secondary sources, social science disciplines, teaching strategies, data interpretation. 6 skills.

Your ADHD advantage: History + Economics = 65% of the exam. These are your two strongest domains by education and passion. If you nail those two, you can pass even if other sections are just okay. Deep study there first — always.

Week 1 — Build the foundation
01
Saturday · Week 1
U.S. History — Colonial Era → American Revolution
25% of examStart strongYour passion area
▶
Block 1 — 40 minutes

Colonial America (1600s–1750s)

  • Reasons for English colonization — religious freedom (Pilgrims/Puritans), economic opportunity, social fresh starts
  • Three colonial regions and their characteristics: New England (religious, trade-based), Middle Colonies (diverse, agricultural), Southern (plantation economy, slavery)
  • Indentured servitude and the development of chattel slavery in the colonies
  • The role of the Mayflower Compact and early self-governance
  • Mercantilism — Britain's economic relationship with the colonies (Navigation Acts)
  • The Great Awakening and its impact on colonial society and religious diversity
  • French and Indian War (1754-1763) — causes, effects, British debt, and seeds of colonial discontent
Block 2 — 40 minutes

The Revolutionary Era (1763–1783)

  • Causes of revolution: Stamp Act, Townshend Acts, Boston Massacre, Boston Tea Party, "taxation without representation"
  • Key figures: George Washington, Thomas Jefferson, Benjamin Franklin, John Adams, Samuel Adams, Thomas Paine (Common Sense)
  • Declaration of Independence (1776) — natural rights philosophy, Locke's influence, key grievances
  • Major battles: Lexington/Concord, Saratoga (turning point), Valley Forge, Yorktown
  • Role of France as an ally — why it mattered for victory
  • Treaty of Paris (1783) — terms, borders, and what the new nation gained
  • Articles of Confederation — what they were, why they failed, what came next
Key terms to know cold
Mercantilism · Salutary Neglect · Natural Rights · Popular Sovereignty · Checks and Balances · Loyalists vs Patriots · Minutemen · Continental Congress · Intolerable Acts · Enlightenment (Locke, Rousseau)

Daily practice: Do 10 U.S. History practice questions at mometrix.com/academy/ftce/social-science-6-12/ — focus on colonial and Revolutionary era questions. Write down every one you get wrong and why.

Sunday · Week 1
U.S. History — Constitution through Civil War
25% of examHigh-yield content
▶
Block 1 — 40 minutes

Constitutional Era and Early Republic (1787–1820s)

  • Constitutional Convention (1787) — major compromises: Great Compromise (House + Senate), Three-Fifths Compromise, Electoral College
  • Federalists vs Anti-Federalists — Hamilton vs Jefferson/Madison on the role of federal government
  • The Bill of Rights — all 10 amendments, why they were added, what each protects
  • Hamilton's economic plan — national bank, assumption of state debts, protective tariffs
  • Louisiana Purchase (1803) — Jefferson's decision, constitutional questions, doubling of U.S. territory
  • War of 1812 — causes, effects, nationalism that followed ("Era of Good Feelings")
  • Monroe Doctrine (1823) — U.S. foreign policy warning to European powers about the Americas
  • Westward expansion — Manifest Destiny ideology, impact on Native Americans
Block 2 — 40 minutes

Antebellum Period through Civil War and Reconstruction (1820–1877)

  • Missouri Compromise (1820), Compromise of 1850, Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854) — how each addressed slavery's expansion
  • Abolitionist movement: Frederick Douglass, William Lloyd Garrison, Harriet Tubman, Underground Railroad
  • Causes of the Civil War: slavery, states' rights, economic differences, election of Lincoln (1860)
  • Key figures: Lincoln, Grant, Lee, Sherman, Stonewall Jackson
  • Key battles: Fort Sumter (start), Antietam (bloodiest), Gettysburg (turning point), Appomattox (end)
  • Emancipation Proclamation (1863) — what it did AND did not do, its strategic purpose
  • 13th, 14th, 15th Amendments — what each guaranteed
  • Reconstruction (1865-1877) — Radical Republicans, Freedmen's Bureau, Black Codes, Jim Crow, Compromise of 1877
Key terms to know cold
Federalism · Nullification Crisis · Sectionalism · Popular Sovereignty · Dred Scott Decision · Secession · Reconstruction Amendments (13th, 14th, 15th) · Sharecropping · Carpetbaggers · Scalawags

Daily practice: 10 practice questions, Civil War era focus. Pay special attention to the causes chain — exam loves asking about Missouri Compromise → Kansas-Nebraska → Dred Scott → election of Lincoln → secession.

Monday · Week 1
U.S. History — Gilded Age through World War II
25% of examHigh density — pace yourself
▶
Block 1 — 40 minutes

Gilded Age, Industrialization, and Immigration (1877–1917)

  • Industrialization — railroads, steel (Carnegie), oil (Rockefeller), "robber barons" vs "captains of industry"
  • Immigration waves (1880s-1920s) — Ellis Island, push/pull factors, nativist backlash, Chinese Exclusion Act
  • Urbanization — rise of cities, tenements, political machines (Tammany Hall)
  • Labor movement — Knights of Labor, AFL (Samuel Gompers), Homestead Strike, Pullman Strike
  • Progressive Era reforms — muckrakers (Upton Sinclair, Ida Tarbell), women's suffrage (19th Amendment 1920), Sherman Antitrust Act
  • Spanish-American War (1898) — U.S. emerges as imperial power, gains Puerto Rico, Guam, Philippines
  • World War I — causes (MAIN: Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism), U.S. enters 1917 (Zimmermann Telegram, unrestricted submarine warfare)
  • Wilson's 14 Points, Treaty of Versailles, U.S. Senate rejects League of Nations
Block 2 — 40 minutes

1920s through World War II (1920–1945)

  • Roaring Twenties — economic boom, Jazz Age, Prohibition, women's new roles, Harlem Renaissance (Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Duke Ellington)
  • Great Depression causes — stock market crash (1929), bank failures, overproduction, Smoot-Hawley tariff
  • New Deal — FDR's relief, recovery, reform programs: Social Security, FDIC, CCC, TVA, WPA
  • Rise of fascism in Europe — Hitler, Mussolini, appeasement policy at Munich
  • U.S. neutrality and path to WWII — Lend-Lease Act, Pearl Harbor (December 7, 1941)
  • WWII key events — D-Day (June 6, 1944), island hopping in Pacific, Holocaust, atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki
  • Home front — rationing, women in workforce (Rosie the Riveter), Japanese American internment (Executive Order 9066)
  • Post-war: United Nations, Marshall Plan, Truman Doctrine, Berlin divided
Key terms to know cold
Social Darwinism · Muckrakers · Progressive Movement · Suffrage · Isolationism · Neutrality Acts · Lend-Lease · Holocaust · Manhattan Project · GI Bill · NATO · Iron Curtain

Daily practice: 10 questions on 1877-1945 content. The New Deal and WWII cause-and-effect chains are exam favorites. Also know the difference between the 1920s boom and the reasons the Depression hit so hard.

Tuesday · Week 1
U.S. History — Cold War, Civil Rights, Contemporary + Florida History
25% of examDon't skip Florida

Block 1 — 40 minutes

Cold War Era and Civil Rights (1945–1980)

  • Cold War framework — containment policy, Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, NATO vs Warsaw Pact
  • Korean War (1950-53) — first "police action," 38th parallel, no formal peace treaty
  • McCarthyism and the Red Scare — HUAC, fear of communist infiltration
  • Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) — closest U.S./USSR came to nuclear war; Kennedy vs Khrushchev
  • Vietnam War — Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, escalation, anti-war movement, Nixon's Vietnamization, Fall of Saigon 1975
  • Civil Rights Movement — Brown v. Board of Education (1954), Montgomery Bus Boycott, March on Washington (1963), Civil Rights Act (1964), Voting Rights Act (1965)
  • Key civil rights figures: MLK Jr., Rosa Parks, Thurgood Marshall, John Lewis, Medgar Evers, Malcolm X
  • Great Society (LBJ) — Medicare, Medicaid, Elementary and Secondary Education Act
Block 2 — 40 minutes

Contemporary Affairs + Florida History

  • 1970s-present: Nixon and Watergate, Reagan Revolution, end of Cold War (Berlin Wall 1989, USSR dissolution 1991)
  • Gulf War (1991), September 11, 2001 and the War on Terror, Iraq and Afghanistan
  • Contemporary issues — immigration debates, economic inequality, globalization, climate change as political issue
  • Florida History (guaranteed on exam):
  • Native peoples — Calusa, Timucua, Seminole tribe and Seminole Wars (3 wars, never fully defeated)
  • Spanish colonization — St. Augustine (1565) is the oldest continuously occupied European settlement in U.S.
  • Florida under Spain, Britain, Spain again, then U.S. territory (1821) — Andrew Jackson as first territorial governor
  • Florida statehood (1845), secession and Civil War role, Reconstruction
  • Early 20th century — cigar industry in Tampa (Ybor City), cattle ranching, citrus agriculture
  • Post-WWII boom — tourism, space program (Cape Canaveral/Kennedy Space Center), Cuban immigration waves
  • Florida's role as a swing state in presidential elections
Key terms to know cold
Containment · Détente · Domino Theory · Nonviolent Resistance · Civil Disobedience · Executive Order 9981 (desegregation of military) · Equal Rights Amendment · Watergate · SALT Treaties · Seminole Wars · St. Augustine · Ybor City

Daily practice: 10 questions, Cold War + Civil Rights focus. Florida history will likely be 2-3 questions — don't ignore it. Know St. Augustine, the Seminoles, and the Cuban immigration connection to Miami.

Wednesday · Week 1
Economics — Your B.Sc. in Exam Format
15% of examStrongest advantage
▶
Block 1 — 40 minutes

Microeconomics Foundations

  • Scarcity — the fundamental economic problem; unlimited wants vs limited resources
  • Opportunity cost — the real cost of any choice is what you give up (not just money)
  • Four economic systems: Traditional (custom-based), Command (government decides), Market (price signals decide), Mixed (combination)
  • The three basic questions every economy must answer: What to produce? How to produce it? For whom?
  • Supply and demand — law of supply (↑ price = ↑ quantity supplied), law of demand (↑ price = ↓ quantity demanded)
  • Market equilibrium — where supply meets demand; what shifts the curves (income, preferences, input costs, technology)
  • Factors of production — land, labor, capital, entrepreneurship
  • Consumer behavior — budgeting, saving, credit, compound interest, opportunity cost of debt
Block 2 — 40 minutes

Macroeconomics and Global Economics

  • GDP (Gross Domestic Product) — measuring total economic output; nominal vs real GDP
  • Business cycle — expansion, peak, recession, trough, recovery
  • Inflation — causes (demand-pull, cost-push), effects on purchasing power, CPI
  • Unemployment — types: frictional, structural, cyclical, seasonal
  • Federal Reserve — the U.S. central bank; controls monetary policy (interest rates, money supply)
  • Fiscal policy vs monetary policy — government taxing/spending vs central bank tools
  • Roles of corporations, labor unions, banks, and stock markets in market economies
  • Global economics: comparative advantage (produce what you do most efficiently), absolute advantage, terms of trade, exchange rates
  • Impacts on less developed countries — dependency theory, IMF, World Bank, WTO roles
Key terms to know cold
Scarcity · Opportunity Cost · Supply/Demand · Equilibrium · Inflation · Deflation · GDP · Monetary Policy · Fiscal Policy · Comparative Advantage · Exchange Rate · Free Trade · Tariff · Subsidy · Laissez-faire · Keynesian Economics

Daily practice: 10 economics practice questions. Your degree gives you a real edge — but watch the exam's framing. Questions often show supply/demand graphs or ask you to identify what shifts a curve. Practice reading those graphs.

Thursday · Week 1
World History — Ancient Civilizations through 1500
25% of examWide scope — stay focused
▶
Block 1 — 40 minutes

Prehistoric Cultures through Classical Civilizations

  • Prehistoric cultures — Paleolithic (hunter-gatherers, tools) vs Neolithic Revolution (agriculture, settlements, specialization)
  • Mesopotamia (Tigris-Euphrates) — Sumerians (cuneiform writing, city-states), Hammurabi's Code (first written law), Babylonian Empire
  • Ancient Egypt — pharaohs, hieroglyphics, mummification, pyramids, Nile River as life source, New Kingdom expansion
  • Indus Valley Civilization — Harappa, Mohenjo-Daro, urban planning, drainage systems, decline still debated
  • Ancient China — Shang dynasty (bronze, writing), Zhou dynasty (Confucianism, Mandate of Heaven), Qin (first unified China, Great Wall), Han dynasty (Silk Road)
  • Ancient Greece — city-states (Athens vs Sparta), democracy in Athens, Persian Wars, Golden Age (Pericles, Socrates, Plato, Aristotle)
  • Alexander the Great — spread of Hellenism across Persia, Egypt, and into India
  • Ancient Rome — republic to empire, Pax Romana, Roman law, roads, aqueducts, spread of Christianity, fall of Western Rome (476 CE)
Block 2 — 40 minutes

Africa, Asia, Mesoamerica, Middle Ages, Renaissance, Reformation (up to 1600)

  • African kingdoms before 1500 — Mali Empire (Mansa Musa, gold/salt trade), Axum (Ethiopia, early Christianity), Great Zimbabwe, Kongo Kingdom
  • Asian societies — Tang and Song dynasties in China (printing, gunpowder, compass), Mughal Empire in India, Japanese feudalism (shogun, samurai, bushido code)
  • Mesoamerica — Maya (calendar, astronomy, writing), Aztec (Tenochtitlan, human sacrifice, tribute system), Inca (Andean road network, terrace farming)
  • Middle Ages (500-1300 CE) — feudalism, Catholic Church's power, Crusades (causes + effects), Black Death (1/3 of Europe dead), Magna Carta (1215)
  • Byzantine Empire — Eastern Roman Empire survives, preserves Greek/Roman knowledge, Eastern Orthodox Christianity
  • Islamic Golden Age — trade routes, algebra, astronomy, medicine (Avicenna), preservation of Greek texts
  • Renaissance (14th-17th c.) — humanism, individual achievement, Italian city-states, Leonardo, Michelangelo, Gutenberg's printing press
  • Protestant Reformation — Martin Luther (95 Theses, 1517), indulgences, sola scriptura, Catholic Counter-Reformation (Council of Trent, Jesuits)
Key terms to know cold
Neolithic Revolution · Cuneiform · Mandate of Heaven · Hellenism · Pax Romana · Feudalism · Crusades · Black Death · Magna Carta · Humanism · Reformation · Indulgences · Counter-Reformation · Silk Road · Mansa Musa

Daily practice: 10 World History questions. The exam draws heavily on comparative questions — "Which civilization was known for X?" Know the unique contribution of each major civilization so you can distinguish them quickly under exam pressure.

Saturday · Week 2
World History — 1500 to 20th Century + World Religions
25% of exam
▶
Block 1 — 40 minutes

Age of Exploration, Enlightenment, and Revolutions (1400s–1800s)

  • Age of Exploration — Portuguese (Vasco da Gama, Bartholomew Dias), Spanish (Columbus, Magellan, Cortés, Pizarro); motivations: God, Gold, Glory
  • Columbian Exchange — what moved from New World to Old World (potatoes, corn, tomatoes, chocolate) and vice versa (horses, cattle, smallpox, wheat)
  • Transatlantic slave trade — Middle Passage, triangle trade, scale of human trafficking, long-term effects on Africa
  • Scientific Revolution — Copernicus (heliocentric), Galileo, Newton; shift from faith-based to evidence-based understanding of nature
  • Age of Enlightenment — Locke (natural rights, social contract), Montesquieu (separation of powers), Rousseau (general will, popular sovereignty), Voltaire (free speech, reason)
  • Age of Revolutions — American Revolution (1776), French Revolution (1789 — causes: debt, inequality, Enlightenment ideas), Haitian Revolution (1791 — only successful slave revolt that created a nation), Latin American independence movements (Bolívar, San Martín)
  • Industrial Revolution (1760-1840) — begins in Britain, steam engine (Watt), textile mills, child labor, urbanization, rise of middle class
Block 2 — 40 minutes

Imperialism, Nationalism, 20th Century Conflicts, and World Religions

  • Imperialism (19th c.) — European powers colonize Africa (Berlin Conference 1884-85 "Scramble for Africa") and Asia; Kipling's "White Man's Burden" ideology
  • Nationalism — the belief that people sharing ethnicity/culture should have their own state; caused both independence movements AND WWI
  • WWI as world history — assassination of Franz Ferdinand, European alliance system (Triple Entente vs Triple Alliance), trench warfare, Ottoman Empire's collapse
  • Russian Revolution (1917) — Bolsheviks, Lenin, overthrow of the Tsar, Marxist-Leninist ideology, formation of USSR
  • Rise of fascism — Mussolini in Italy, Hitler in Germany (National Socialism, Holocaust — 6 million Jews, 5 million others), totalitarianism vs democracy
  • WWII as world history — European theater, Pacific theater, D-Day, atomic bombs, Nuremberg Trials, UN Charter
  • Cold War global effects — proxy wars, decolonization movement (India 1947, African independence movements 1950s-60s), Non-Aligned Movement
  • World Religions: Christianity (salvation through Christ, Bible), Islam (Five Pillars, Quran, Muhammad), Judaism (Torah, covenant, monotheism), Hinduism (dharma, karma, reincarnation, Vedas), Buddhism (Four Noble Truths, Eightfold Path, Siddhartha Gautama)
Key terms to know cold
Columbian Exchange · Middle Passage · Enlightenment · Social Contract · Imperialism · Nationalism · Bolshevism · Fascism · Holocaust · Decolonization · Five Pillars · Four Noble Truths · Karma · Dharma · Covenant

Daily practice: 10 World History questions. Religion questions will be comparative — "Which religion teaches X?" Know the core beliefs of each major faith and their geographic origins. Don't confuse Sunni/Shia (Islam) or Protestant/Catholic distinctions.

Sunday · Week 2
Political Science — Constitution Deep Dive
15% of examConnects to U.S. History
▶
Block 1 — 40 minutes

The U.S. Constitution and Structure of Government

  • Constitutional principles: popular sovereignty, limited government, separation of powers, checks and balances, federalism, republicanism, individual rights
  • The Bill of Rights — all 10 amendments in plain English (1st: speech/religion/press; 2nd: arms; 4th: searches; 5th: self-incrimination; 6th: fair trial; etc.)
  • Key amendments beyond the Bill of Rights: 13th (abolished slavery), 14th (equal protection/due process), 15th (Black male vote), 19th (women's vote), 22nd (presidential term limits), 26th (18 year old vote)
  • Separation of powers: Legislative (Article I — Congress makes laws), Executive (Article II — President enforces laws), Judicial (Article III — courts interpret laws)
  • Checks and balances — veto, override, judicial review (Marbury v. Madison), impeachment, Senate confirmation
  • Federalism — powers reserved to states (10th Amendment), concurrent powers, supremacy clause
  • State and local government — governor, state legislature, city councils, school boards
Block 2 — 40 minutes

Elections, Foreign Policy, and Comparative Government

  • U.S. electoral process — primary elections, Electoral College (how it works, why it exists, controversies), winner-take-all vs proportional allocation
  • Political parties — two-party system, platforms, party realignment (Solid South, Reagan Democrats), third parties and why they rarely succeed
  • Interest groups, lobbying, PACs and Super PACs, Citizens United decision
  • Public opinion — polling, media influence, agenda-setting theory, framing
  • U.S. foreign policy principles — isolationism vs interventionism, Monroe Doctrine, Truman Doctrine, containment, détente, neoconservatism
  • Rights, privileges, and responsibilities of U.S. citizenship — natural-born vs naturalized, jury duty, voting, paying taxes, military service
  • Comparative government systems: democracy (presidential vs parliamentary), monarchy (constitutional vs absolute), authoritarianism, totalitarianism, theocracy
  • Know examples: UK (parliamentary democracy), China (one-party authoritarian), Saudi Arabia (absolute monarchy/theocracy), France (semi-presidential)
Key terms to know cold
Checks and Balances · Federalism · Judicial Review · Electoral College · Lobbying · Filibuster · Due Process · Equal Protection · Sovereignty · Theocracy · Oligarchy · Bicameral · Concurrent Powers · Supremacy Clause

Daily practice: 10 Political Science questions. The exam often presents scenarios — "A state passes a law that conflicts with federal law; what governs?" Answer: supremacy clause. Know your constitutional doctrines well enough to apply them to scenarios you've never seen before.

Monday · Week 2
Geography + Social Science Methodology
10% + 10% = 20% of examEfficient combo day
▶
Block 1 — 40 minutes

Geography — Six Essential Elements and Core Concepts

  • The Six Essential Elements of Geography (memorize all six): 1. The World in Spatial Terms, 2. Places and Regions, 3. Physical Systems, 4. Human Systems, 5. Environment and Society, 6. The Uses of Geography
  • Five Themes of Geography (also appears on exam): Location (absolute vs relative), Place (physical and human characteristics), Region (formal, functional, perceptual), Movement (people, goods, ideas), Human-Environment Interaction
  • Physical geography — plate tectonics, erosion, river systems, climate zones (tropical, desert, temperate, polar), biomes
  • Human geography — population distribution, demographic transition model, urbanization, cultural diffusion
  • Human-environment interaction — how humans adapt to and modify environments (deforestation, irrigation, urbanization, climate change)
  • Reading maps: latitude/longitude, map projections (Mercator distortion), thematic maps, topographic maps, choropleth maps
  • Reading geographic data — interpreting tables, graphs, and charts showing population, climate, trade data
Block 2 — 40 minutes

Social Science Methodology — Disciplines, Sources, and Teaching

  • Social science disciplines: Anthropology (human cultures, physical evolution), Sociology (social groups, institutions, behavior), Psychology (individual mind and behavior), Political Science (government, power), Economics (resource allocation), History (past events and causation), Geography (space and place)
  • Interrelationships — how the disciplines overlap: sociology and anthropology both study culture; economics and political science intersect in public policy
  • Key social science concepts: culture, socialization, social class, race as social construct, gender roles, technology's social impact
  • Primary sources — original, firsthand materials created at the time of study: letters, diaries, photographs, speeches, treaties, political cartoons, artifacts
  • Secondary sources — created after the fact, analyzing primary sources: textbooks, encyclopedias, documentaries, biographies, journal articles
  • Interpreting political cartoons — symbolism, exaggeration, caricature; always identify what the cartoonist is commenting on
  • Teaching social science — inquiry-based learning, using primary sources in the classroom, simulations and role-play, current events integration, DBQ (Document-Based Questions)
  • Reading social science data — bar graphs, pie charts, data tables, timelines; distinguish correlation from causation
Key terms to know cold
Six Essential Elements · Absolute vs Relative Location · Demographic Transition · Cultural Diffusion · Anthropology · Sociology · Primary Source · Secondary Source · DBQ · Socialization · Social Stratification · Map Projection

Daily practice: 10 questions split between Geography and Methodology. Geography questions often include maps or graphs — practice reading them quickly. Methodology questions are often the easiest on the exam — don't overthink them.

Exam Day
You Are Ready. Go Execute.
Game dayTrust your prep
▶
Exam day protocol

Morning of exam

  • Wake up with enough time — no rushing. Eat a real breakfast (protein + carbs; no heavy or unfamiliar foods)
  • Light review ONLY if you feel you need it — 10 minutes max of your "keep forgetting" list. Then put your notes away.
  • Arrive at the testing center 20-30 minutes early (Pearson VUE centers require check-in procedures)
  • Bring: two valid government-issued IDs (e.g., driver's license + passport or credit card with signature)
  • No personal items allowed in the testing room — lockers are provided

During the exam

  • Read EVERY question carefully — especially any with "EXCEPT," "NOT," or "BEST describes"
  • Answer every question — no penalty for guessing. Never leave blank.
  • If stuck: eliminate 1-2 clearly wrong answers, pick your best guess, FLAG it, and move on — return at the end
  • Aim for 1 min 45 sec per question; at the 90-minute mark you should be around question 50
  • Trust your first instinct — research shows that changing answers usually hurts ADHD test-takers more than it helps
  • Your score appears IMMEDIATELY when you finish. Breathe.

Remember this

  • You have taught young people for 15+ years. You know how to explain, inspire, and lead. That is what this test is ultimately measuring.

ADHD-specific study strategies

⏱

Pomodoro method

25 min study, 5 min break. Repeat 4 times, then take a 20-min break. Use a physical timer — your phone has too many distractions. This matches your two 40-min study blocks perfectly.

🎙

Teach it back out loud

After each block, close your notes and explain the topic out loud as if teaching your future students. You already do this naturally in ministry. If you can teach it, you'll remember it on the exam.

📌

Sticky note system

One sticky note per concept you keep forgetting. Put them on your bathroom mirror, car dashboard, and refrigerator. You'll review them passively 10+ times a day without even trying.

🏆

Reward every session

After every completed study block, do something enjoyable for 10 min — walk outside, coffee, read a history chapter you love. Your brain needs the dopamine hit to sustain effort across 14 days.

📱

Accommodation request

File an Alternative Testing Arrangement Request at fl.nesinc.com with ADHD documentation from your doctor. Even 25% extra time = 37 bonus minutes. Do this today — not the week before.

🔗

Connect to your calling

When motivation drops, remember: this is the gateway to your classroom. Every concept you master is one you'll bring to life for students who need a teacher exactly like you. This is your calling.

Exam-day test-taking strategies

Time management

80 questions in 150 minutes = 1 min 52 sec per question. Flag any question you're unsure about and keep moving. Return to flagged questions after finishing.

Watch for "EXCEPT" and "NOT"

The exam loves these trap words. Read every question twice. Underline the key verb. ADHD brains tend to skim — consciously slow down when you see EXCEPT, NOT, or BEST.

Elimination strategy

Even if you don't know the right answer, you can usually eliminate 1-2 obviously wrong choices. That turns a 25% guess into 50%. Always guess — no penalty for wrong answers.

Trust your first instinct

Research shows that for ADHD test-takers, changing answers typically hurts more than it helps. Go with your gut unless you have a specific reason to change. Don't second-guess yourself to death.

Scores are immediate

You'll see pass/fail the moment you finish. Official score report comes within 4 weeks. Passing score = 200 scaled. If you don't pass, you can retake after 31 days — but you're going to pass.

Scope management

History + Economics = 65% of the exam. If you feel behind on World History or Political Science, prioritize the big sections. A perfect History + Economics score can carry you through weaker areas.
